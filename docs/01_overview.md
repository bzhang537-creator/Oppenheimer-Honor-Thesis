# Overview & Motivation

[← back to README](../README.md)

## The setting

J. Robert Oppenheimer sat at the center of American theoretical physics from the 1930s through
the 1950s. Three political shocks reshaped his position:

1. **1942 — Manhattan Project.** Oppenheimer is recruited to lead the weapons laboratory at Los
   Alamos. He and much of his network are pulled out of open academic publishing into classified
   wartime work.
2. **1949 — Soviet atomic test.** The Soviet bomb reframes Oppenheimer, and physicists associated
   with him, as objects of security suspicion during the early Cold War.
3. **1954 — security clearance revocation.** After a public hearing, Oppenheimer's clearance is
   revoked, formalizing his political fall.

## The question

Did these shocks propagate through Oppenheimer's **scientific co-authorship network** and suppress
the research productivity of the physicists tied to him? Productivity is measured by annual
*Physical Review* publication counts over 1931–1960.

Treatment is defined by **pre-1942 network distance** from Oppenheimer, giving three concentric
exposure tiers:

- **E1** — 11 direct co-authors
- **E2** — 107 co-authors of co-authors
- **E3** — 85 IAS Princeton physicists (1947–54)

against a control group of 24 solid-state and acoustics physicists with no network connection.

## The three sub-questions

The analysis asks whether the productivity effect:

1. **exists** at all,
2. **attenuates with network distance** (closer ties, larger effect), and
3. reflects **temporary incapacitation** (a dip that recovers) or a **persistent chilling effect**
   (a decline that does not recover).

The distinction in (3) is the heart of the thesis. Wartime incapacitation predicts a dip in
1942–45 that rebounds once physicists return from Los Alamos. A political/chilling channel predicts
suppression that appears with the 1949 shock and does *not* recover. Separating these is what the
[Manhattan benchmark](06_benchmark.md) is built to do.

## Why it matters

The setting is a natural experiment in how political pressure on a central node affects the
productivity of an entire scientific network — a question with echoes in the economics of science
(Azoulay, Graff Zivin & Wang 2010; Waldinger 2010; Borjas & Doran 2012) and in contemporary debates
about political interference in research.
